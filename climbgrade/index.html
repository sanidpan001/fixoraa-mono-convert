Why Climbing Has Three Grading Languages

Ask a climber in Yosemite, Fontainebleau, and the Dolomites how hard a route is and you will get three different answers for the same climb. The Yosemite Decimal System (YDS) dominates North America, the French system rules most of Europe and much of the world, and the UIAA scale persists in Germany, Austria, and alpine tradition. They all measure the same thing — the difficulty of the single hardest move, assuming good conditions — but they slice the scale differently.

The Yosemite Decimal System (YDS)

Born in the 1950s from the Sierra Club's 1-to-5 classification (where 5 meant 'technical rock climbing'), YDS subdivides class 5 into 5.0 through 5.15, with letter suffixes a–d splitting grades from 5.10 upward. So 5.10a is easier than 5.10d, and 5.11a is a full step harder than any 5.10. The open-ended top (currently 5.15d) keeps expanding as human limits move.

Roughly translated to experience: 5.7–5.8 is a confident beginner's playground, 5.9–5.10a is the intermediate heartland where most gym climbers live, 5.10b–5.11b marks the advanced zone requiring real training, and 5.12+ is expert territory demanding dedicated coaching and conditioning.

The French System

The French scale runs 1 to 9 with letter subdivisions a, b, c and '+' modifiers: 6a, 6a+, 6b, 6b+, and so on. It is arguably the most logical of the three — each step is a fairly even increment — and it has become the de facto international language of sport climbing. A French 6a corresponds to YDS 5.10a; 7a+ maps to 5.12a. Because the steps are finer than YDS number jumps, French grades are often preferred for describing progress: moving from 6b to 6b+ feels like a real, nameable achievement.

The UIAA Scale

The International Climbing and Mountaineering Federation's scale runs from I to XII with − and + modifiers (VI−, VI, VI+). It originated in alpine climbing, where grades had to describe long mountain routes, and it remains standard in German-speaking countries and much of Eastern Europe. UIAA VII+ equals French 6c and YDS 5.11a. The scale's alpine roots mean it sometimes weights route-finding and seriousness alongside pure move difficulty — a nuance worth knowing when comparing guidebooks.

Treat conversions as approximate within half a grade. Grading is subjective — set by the first ascensionist and debated forever after — and style matters enormously. A 6b slab and a 6b overhang can feel like different sports.

What About Bouldering Grades?

Bouldering uses separate scales: the V-scale (V0–V17) in North America and the Font scale (4–8C+) almost everywhere else. There is no clean formula mapping a route grade to a boulder grade, because a route's difficulty includes endurance. As a very rough equivalence, the crux of a 5.12a might feel like V4–V5 climbing — but a V5 boulderer with no endurance training can still fall off a 5.11a route. Convert between V and Font with a dedicated bouldering chart rather than forcing it through this one.

Indoor vs. Outdoor Grades

Gym grades are famously soft — or 'gym-soft,' as climbers say. Commercial gyms grade generously to keep customers progressing and happy, and plastic holds offer perfect friction. Expect your outdoor grade to lag one to two full number grades behind your gym grade at first. The gap closes with experience reading real rock, placing protection, and managing fear, which are skills no plastic can teach.

How to Break Into the Next Grade

  • Climb more, spray less: volume at your current limit beats occasional heroic attempts two grades up.
  • Learn to rest: the difference between 5.10 and 5.11 is often the ability to shake out on a mediocre hold for 30 seconds.
  • Footwork first: quiet feet — placing each foot precisely once, without scraping — is the cheapest grade gain in climbing.
  • Project smart: pick a route one grade above your redpoint level, work the moves in sections, then link them. That is how 5.11a becomes 5.11b.
  • Train the antagonist muscles: push-ups, reverse wrist curls, and shoulder stabilisers keep you climbing while your pulling muscles get stronger.

A Final Word on Safety

Grades describe difficulty, not danger. A well-protected 5.10 sport route can be safer than a runout 5.7 trad line. Learn to assess protection quality, anchor building, and retreat options separately from the grade — and take instruction from a certified guide or instructor before leading outdoors. The converter above will translate the numbers; only experience and training translate them into good judgment.

Grade Milestones: What Each Band Demands From You

Easy (5.7–5.9 / 5a–5c): the focus is entirely on movement fundamentals — quiet feet, straight arms, and learning to trust your feet on small holds. Most people arrive here within their first month. The milestone that matters is not the grade but the first clean, controlled top-out where nothing felt desperate.

Intermediate (5.10a–5.10c / 6a–6b): technique starts separating from strength. The key new skills are resting efficiently on the route (finding the shake-out stance instead of powering through), reading sequences before leaving the ground, and clipping from stable positions. This is the longest band — many climbers spend years here, and that is normal and enjoyable.

Advanced (5.11a–5.11b / 6c–6c+): structured training enters the picture. Finger strength becomes the limiter for most people, so hangboard work, careful load management, and antagonist training (push-ups, wrist extensors) matter as much as climbing itself. Projecting — working a route over multiple sessions — becomes the standard way to climb at your limit.

Expert (5.12a+ / 7a+ and up): everything is optimised: periodised training blocks, nutrition, sleep, skin management, and mental training for falling and performing under pressure. Gains come slowly and injuries must be managed proactively. Coaching is nearly universal at this level because self-coached plateaus are the norm.

Benchmark Routes That Define the Grades

Certain famous climbs have become informal calibration points that climbers reference worldwide:

  • 5.9: routes like Reed's Pinnacle Direct (Yosemite) — the classic benchmark for the grade.
  • 5.10a: Snake Dike (Half Dome) — technically moderate but a serious, exposed adventure.
  • 5.11a: Equinox (Joshua Tree) — the gateway grade's famous exam.
  • 5.12a: To Bolt or Not to Be (Smith Rock) — a rite of passage for the expert threshold.
  • 5.15a / 9a+: Biographie (Céuse, France) — a legendary test piece at the absolute limit.

Climbing one of these is worth a dozen gym laps at the same nominal grade — outdoor benchmarks keep your sense of the scale honest.

A Note on Grading Ethics: Sandbags and Soft Touches

Every climbing area has its folklore: the 'sandbagged' crag where 5.10a feels like 5.11b, and the 'soft' gym where grades flatter. Both exist, and both have causes. Older traditional areas were graded by pioneers climbing in worse gear with bolder ethics, and grades calcified out of respect. Newer sport crags graded by consensus tend to be more accurate. Neither is 'wrong' — grading is intersubjective by nature. The practical takeaway: treat the first few routes at any new area as calibration climbs, and never let a number override what your body tells you. A wise climber once said the best climber is the one having the most fun, and no sandbag can take that grade away from you.

The British Trad Grades: E-Grades and the Technical Grade

British trad climbing uses a system no converter handles cleanly, because it grades two things at once. The adjectival grade (E1–E11, following Moderate through Extremely Severe) describes overall seriousness — difficulty, protection quality, exposure, and commitment combined. The technical grade (4a–7b, French-style) describes the single hardest move. A route is written as E1 5b: adjectival first, technical second.

This dual system explains why conversions feel approximate: E1 5b roughly corresponds to 5.10a, E4 6a to about 5.11c/5.12a — but the E-grade also encodes how dangerous a fall would be, something YDS and French grades ignore entirely. A well-protected E2 can feel easier than a bold, runout E1 at the same technical grade. If you climb trad in the UK, learn to read both halves: the technical grade tells you whether you can do the moves, and the E-grade tells you whether you should try.

A Worked Example: Translating Your Grade for a Trip Abroad

Say you comfortably redpoint 5.11b at your home crag in Colorado and you are planning a sport-climbing trip to Kalymnos, Greece, where the guidebook speaks French. Convert first: 5.11b maps to 6c+ (UIAA VIII−). On day one, do not jump on 6c+ routes — pick 6a+ to 6b+ as calibration climbs to learn the limestone style, which rewards delicate footwork over the power your sandstone crag trained. Once the style clicks, project a 6c or 6c+ that suits your strengths.

Then sanity-check with the outdoor-discount rule: if your 5.11b is a gym grade, treat it as 5.10b outdoors (French 6a+) until proven otherwise. Keep the conversion table on your phone, and when a local says a route is 'soft for the grade', believe them — then add half a grade of scepticism anyway. Grades travel badly; humility travels well.

Frequently asked questions

Which climbing grade system should I learn?

YDS (e.g. 5.10a) is standard in North America, the French system (e.g. 6a) dominates Europe and international sport climbing, and UIAA (e.g. VI+) is used in German-speaking and alpine regions. They describe the same difficulty; use the converter above to translate between them.

What is the difference between 5.10a and 5.10b?

In YDS, 5.10a is the easiest subdivision of 5.10 and 5.10d the hardest, so 5.10b is one letter-step harder than 5.10a. In French terms that is roughly 6a versus 6a+ — a noticeable but small increment, like the difference between cruising and having to try.

What is the hardest climbing grade in the world?

As of recent records, 5.15d (French 9a+) is the hardest confirmed redpoint grade in the world, first climbed by Adam Ondra on Silence in 2017. Only a handful of climbers on Earth operate at 5.15c and above.

How do bouldering grades convert to route grades?

Bouldering uses its own scales — V-scale (V0–V17) in the US and Font scale (4–8C+) elsewhere — and there is no exact formula converting them to route grades, because routes add endurance to the equation. Roughly, the crux moves of a 5.12a feel like V4–V5 bouldering.

Why does the French system use letters like 6a+?

The French system uses numbers 1–9 with letter subdivisions a/b/c and '+' modifiers (6a, 6a+, 6b…), giving finer, more even steps than YDS. It became the international standard for sport climbing because it describes progress precisely and translates cleanly across countries.

Are indoor climbing grades the same as outdoor?

Indoor gym grades typically run one to two grades softer than outdoor grades — gyms grade generously and plastic offers perfect friction. If you climb 5.11a in the gym, expect to start around 5.9–5.10a on real rock until you learn to read stone, manage fear, and place protection.
