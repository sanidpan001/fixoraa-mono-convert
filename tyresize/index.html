Reading Your Tyre's Sidewall

Every tyre carries its dimensions moulded into the sidewall in a code like 205/55R16 91V. Once you learn to read it, you can shop for replacements, compare upgrades, and understand exactly what is bolted to your car:

  • 205 — the tread width in millimetres, measured sidewall to sidewall.
  • 55 — the aspect ratio: the sidewall height as a percentage of the width. Here the sidewall is 55% of 205 mm, or 112.75 mm.
  • R — radial construction (virtually all modern car tyres).
  • 16 — the wheel rim diameter in inches that the tyre fits.
  • 91 — the load index: how much weight each tyre can carry (91 = 615 kg).
  • V — the speed rating: the maximum sustained speed (V = 240 km/h / 149 mph).

From Markings to Real Dimensions

The three sizing numbers fully determine the tyre's geometry. The sidewall height is simply width × aspect ratio ÷ 100, and the overall diameter adds two sidewalls to the rim:

Sidewall = width × aspect ÷ 100
Diameter = rim + 2 × sidewall ÷ 25.4 (in inches)

For a 205/55R16: sidewall = 205 × 0.55 = 112.75 mm, and diameter = 16 + 2 × 112.75 ÷ 25.4 = 24.88 inches (631.9 mm). The circumference — π × diameter — is what actually matters for your speedometer: about 78.2 inches per revolution for this size.

Why Diameter Matters: Speedometer and Odometer Error

Your car's speedometer does not measure speed directly. It counts wheel revolutions and multiplies by the factory tyre's circumference. Fit a tyre with a different overall diameter and the maths breaks: a larger diameter means each revolution covers more ground, so you travel faster than the speedometer shows. A smaller diameter reads fast — you are going slower than indicated.

The industry rule of thumb is to keep the overall diameter within ±3% of the original size. At 3% on a 70 mph speedometer reading, the real speed differs by about 2 mph — small, but enough to matter for speeding tickets, and the odometer error accumulates into service-interval and resale-mileage discrepancies. Plus-Sizing: Bigger Wheels, Same Diameter

Plus-sizing means fitting a larger wheel with a lower-profile tyre so the overall diameter stays nearly identical — for example, moving from 205/55R16 to 225/45R17. Done correctly, the speedometer stays accurate while you gain sharper handling and sportier looks from the shorter, stiffer sidewall.

Load Index and Speed Rating

These two codes are safety-critical and often ignored. The load index must meet or exceed your vehicle's placard rating (found on the driver's door jamb) — fitting a lower index is illegal in many jurisdictions and can void insurance. Common values: 91 = 615 kg, 94 = 670 kg, 98 = 750 kg per tyre.

The speed rating must also meet the manufacturer's minimum. The common letters in ascending order: T (190 km/h), H (210 km/h), V (240 km/h), W (270 km/h), Y (300 km/h). A higher rating than stock is fine; a lower one is not, even if you never drive that fast — insurers and inspectors treat it as a safety downgrade.

Can You Mix Tyre Sizes?

On the same axle, no — mismatched sizes across an axle destabilise handling and confuse ABS and stability control. All-wheel-drive vehicles are especially sensitive: even different tread depths front-to-rear can overwork the centre differential, which is why some manufacturers specify replacing all four tyres together. A space-saver spare is the engineered exception, and it comes with strict speed and distance limits for exactly this reason.

When to Replace: Tread Depth and Age

Legal minimum tread depth is 1.6 mm across most of Europe and 2/32 in (1.6 mm) in most US states, but wet-weather braking degrades badly below 3 mm — many safety organisations recommend replacing at 3–4 mm. Check the DOT date code on the sidewall (a four-digit week/year like '3223' = week 32 of 2023): most manufacturers recommend replacement at 6–10 years regardless of tread, because rubber compounds harden and crack with age even on a garaged car.

Winter Tyres and Diameter

A popular winter strategy is 'minus-sizing': a narrower tyre on a smaller wheel (e.g., 195/65R15 instead of 205/55R16) that keeps the same overall diameter. The narrower tread cuts through slush and snow to find grip, the taller sidewall shrugs off potholes, and smaller wheels and tyres cost less. Just verify brake-caliper clearance on the smaller wheel before committing.

Bottom line: the sidewall code is a complete engineering specification, not decoration. Decode it with the tool above before you buy, keep diameter changes inside ±3%, never downgrade load or speed ratings, and your car will handle, brake, and read speed exactly as its engineers intended.

Where to Find Your Correct Size

Before comparing alternatives, confirm what your car actually requires. The definitive source is the tyre placard on the driver's door jamb — it lists the factory size, load index, and pressures for front and rear. The owner's manual carries the same data plus approved alternatives. What is moulded on your current tyres only tells you what is fitted now, which may differ from stock if a previous owner changed sizes. When in doubt, the placard wins: it is what the manufacturer engineered, tested, and certified.

Also check the spare: space-saver spares are narrower and smaller by design and carry strict limits (usually 80 km/h and limited distance). A full-size matching spare has no such limits but needs the same load and speed ratings as the other four.

Staggered Fitments, Directional Tyres, and Rotation

Some performance cars run staggered fitments — wider tyres at the rear than the front. You cannot rotate these front-to-rear, and you must compare each axle against its own stock size, not against the other axle. Directional tyres (marked with a rotation arrow) must roll in one direction for their tread pattern to evacuate water correctly; fitting one backwards measurably worsens wet braking. Asymmetric tyres (marked 'outside') can go on either side of the car but must be mounted with the correct face out.

Rotation patterns depend on the setup: non-directional, same-size tyres rotate in a cross or front-to-rear pattern every 8,000–12,000 km to even out wear; directional tyres stay on the same side. Uneven wear is also a diagnostic tool — centre wear means over-inflation, edge wear means under-inflation, and one-sided wear points to alignment issues. Check pressures monthly when cold, because a tyre can be 20% under-inflated with no visible sag on modern stiff sidewalls.

Tyre Pressure: The Free Performance Upgrade

Before spending money on new tyres, check your pressures — it is the single highest-value maintenance on any car. Under-inflation by just 20% increases rolling resistance (hurting fuel economy by ~3%), accelerates edge wear, lengthens braking distances, and raises the risk of heat-related blowouts at motorway speeds. Over-inflation does the opposite damage: a harsh ride, reduced grip patch, and rapid centre wear. The correct pressures are on the door-jamb placard, not the tyre sidewall (which shows the maximum, not the recommendation). Check monthly when the tyres are cold, and always before long trips or when carrying heavy loads — most placards list a higher 'loaded' pressure for exactly that.

Worked Example: Checking an Upgrade Before You Buy

Your placard says 205/55R16 and you are tempted by a 225/45R17 wheel-and-tyre package. First compute the stock diameter: sidewall = 205 × 0.55 = 112.75 mm; diameter = 16 + 2 × 112.75 ÷ 25.4 = 24.88 in. The candidate: sidewall = 225 × 0.45 = 101.25 mm; diameter = 17 + 2 × 101.25 ÷ 25.4 = 24.97 in. The change is (24.97 − 24.88) ÷ 24.88 = +0.4% — comfortably inside the ±3% rule.

Translate that to the speedometer: at an indicated 70 mph you are actually travelling 70 × 24.97 ÷ 24.88 ≈ 70.3 mph, an error no camera will notice. Now check the other two non-negotiables: the new tyre's load index must meet or exceed the placard (91 = 615 kg per tyre here), and the speed rating must be V or higher if the placard says V. Diameter passes, load passes, rating passes — the upgrade is sound. Run the same arithmetic on any package a shop offers you before paying.

Insurance, Inspections, and the Law

Diameter is physics; the paperwork is separate. In many countries any wheel or tyre change away from the manufacturer's approved sizes counts as a modification you must declare to your insurer — an undeclared change can void cover after a claim, even if the tyres had nothing to do with the crash. Periodic inspections vary too: Germany's TÜV requires an engineer's sign-off for non-approved sizes, while the UK's MOT mainly checks that tyres are correctly seated, undamaged, and adequately rated. Keep a photo of the placard on your phone and receipts showing the new size's load and speed ratings — when a seller says 'these will fit fine', verify with the calculator above, because 'fit' and 'legal' are different questions.

Frequently asked questions

What does 205/55R16 mean?

205 is the tread width in mm, 55 is the aspect ratio (sidewall height = 55% of 205 = 112.75 mm), R means radial construction, and 16 is the wheel rim diameter in inches. Overall diameter is 24.88 inches (631.9 mm).

Can I fit bigger tyres on my car?

You can, but keep the overall diameter within ±3% of the original so the speedometer, odometer, gearing, and clearances stay correct. Also verify the new width clears the suspension and arches at full steering lock, and never fit a lower load index or speed rating than stock.

How much speedometer error is acceptable with different tyres?

Up to ±3% overall diameter change is the widely accepted safe limit. Beyond that, speedometer and odometer readings become meaningfully wrong, gearing and ESC calibration suffer, and the tyre may rub. Use the comparison tool above to check any swap before buying.

What is the load index on a tyre?

The load index (e.g. 91) is the maximum weight each tyre can carry — 91 equals 615 kg. Your replacement tyres must have a load index equal to or higher than the vehicle manufacturer's placard rating on the driver's door jamb.

What does the speed rating letter mean?

The speed rating letter (T, H, V, W, Y…) is the maximum sustained speed the tyre is certified for: H = 210 km/h, V = 240 km/h. You may fit a higher rating than stock but never a lower one — it is a safety downgrade that can affect insurance and inspections.

Can I mix different tyre sizes on my car?

No — different sizes on the same axle destabilise handling and confuse ABS and stability control. On all-wheel-drive cars even uneven tread wear front-to-rear can damage the centre differential. Always replace axle pairs together, and ideally all four on AWD.
